# Voice

How Bhuvanesh writes, measured from his own text, and the machine patterns to keep out of anything
the Desk drafts. Every number below comes from scripts run over his writing; the method and corpus
sizes are at the end. The machine-usable version lives in `packages/shared/src/voice.ts`.

Read this as a mirror, not a rulebook. Nothing here is applied to your words: the Desk never rewrites
the editor. It describes you so that machine drafts sit next to your notes without sounding like a
press release, and so you can see your own habits clearly.

## The register in a line

A well-read friend telling you what he found this week: plain, specific Indian English in sentences
of about fifteen words, first person, funny and crude in passing, praising what he loved a little too
often, and handing over to the quoted source with a colon.

The written curatorial voice is the one that matters for From the Dumpster Fire: "why it's here"
notes, the field note, Embers and Rhymes captions. The dictated essay voice is what your voice notes
sound like before cleanup, and it is where the long, rolling sentences live. The working voice is how
you talk to tools; it matters to the Desk only for transcription hints.

## The curatorial voice

### Rhythm

Your written sentences are medium and even. The median is 15 words, the middle 80% run from 6 to 26
words, and only one sentence in twenty passes 30 words. Paragraphs are short: 2.5 sentences on
average, and 38% are a single sentence. A long build followed by a short punch happens about once per
100 sentences, so when it comes it lands:

> So I set up this section of the blog to read a poem a week and share it, hoping that a wayward
> traveler on the internet might stumble upon one and be inspired to read poetry like I was. I failed.

> Another thing I've been thinking about a lot over the past year is that there seems to be a
> widespread feeling of a lack of meaning and disconnection among people. I don't know why.

You almost never stack short sentences. Runs of three or more sentences of five words or fewer occur
0.4 times per 10,000 words, and most are paired opposites ("Fear and greed. Hope and despair.").

### Imagery

You reach for the ordinary and the local. Similes with "like a" appear 87 times (6.5 per 10,000
words), "as X as" 73 times, and the pictures come from Bangalore streets, bodily functions and pop
culture far more often than from myth (myth and gods: 4.1 per 10,000 words in the notes, against 46
in the Odyssey and AI essays).

> The interweb is like a smelly public toilet with no doors near the Majestic Bus Stand.

> A few weeks ago, I published this post over the weekend and then went to sleep like a BBMP dog.

Lists run long when the point is excess: 260 lists of four or more items (19.5 per 10,000 words).

> It was a beautiful Thursday morning in the smelly, water-starved, garbage-ridden, gridlocked,
> treeless garden city that is Namma Bengaluru.

### Moves

The core curatorial move is the handover. 515 paragraphs (15% of all paragraphs) end with a colon
that introduces a quotation, and you name exactly who said it and where. Around the quote you tell
the reader how you found it and what it connected to:

- "I came across" 42 times, "reminds me of" or "reminded me of" 51, "I loved" 38, "Pair this with" 21.
- "Here's" 107 times, almost always as a plain handover ("Here's an excerpt from..."), rarely as a
  drum-roll.
- Honest uncertainty is frequent: "I don't know" 37 times, plus "I could be wrong", "I'm not sure",
  "to be fair" (45 in all).

### Vocabulary

Measured against general English frequencies, your over-used words (names excluded) are those of a
media and finance reader with a fondness for internet slang: interweb, pessimists, optimists, moar,
performative, consumerism, dopamine, crises, arbitrage, newsletters, publishers, podcasts, rabbit
holes, existential, nuance, banger. Two families stand out:

- Praise words: brilliant (110), fascinating (70), interesting (69), amazing (58), wonderful (54),
  beautiful (46), profound (45), delightful (17).
- Self-deprecation: dumb, stupid, idiot, "remarkably average", "a little less dumb" (92 in all,
  6.9 per 10,000 words).

Spelling leans American: "color", "favorite" and "behavior" outnumber the British forms 117 to 7. Indian English shows mostly as place and texture
(Bangalore, BBMP, Silk Board, gyan, chaddi) in the notes; rupees, lakh and crore arrive with the 2026
finance essays.

### Punctuation

Commas carry the prose (54 per 1,000 words). You use the em dash, unspaced, about once every 460
words, and you did so at the same rate in early 2022, before ChatGPT existed (24 per 10,000 words
in 2022, 19 to 21 in 2023 and 2024). Colons are common (5.3 per 1,000) because of the handover.
Semicolons are rare (0.8 per 1,000), parentheses moderate (2.0), exclamation marks rare (0.4).

> The term perfectly captures the essence of this blog as well—always exploring, never done.

### Openings and closings

Of 62 posts, 28 open with how you came to the subject ("Last week, Sam Harris' conversation with
Rich Roll popped up on my podcast feed."), 24 open with a blunt claim, image or question ("Things
have never been better.", "There's a weird molecule in your brain."), 6 open by apologising for the
post ("This is going to be a messy post, so brace yourself."), and 4 are early digest openers.

Closings: 18 end on a comic or imperative send-off, 11 on a question to the reader ("What did you
think?"), 4 on "That's it for this week", and the rest on the last link of a list. None ends on a
moral.

> Go and suffer a little.

> Now eat Lays chips, fart, and make someone laugh.

### Humour

Crude, deadpan and frequent in the notes: swearing 238 times (17.9 per 10,000 words), farts and
toilets 108 times. The jokes undercut whatever you just said seriously.

> If you went to SoftBank or Tiger with an idea about an app that analyzes farts and tells what flower
> or vegetable they smell like, you'd be assaulted with a $100 million cheque—FSaaS: fart smelling as
> a service.

In the 2026 finance essays the swearing falls to about 1.5 per 10,000 words. That is a register
choice for Zerodha-adjacent writing, and the Dumpster Fire can have the old licence back.

### Titles

Short (5.6 words on average), concrete and wry, often a pun or a twisted cliché; 3 of 62 use a colon
and 3 are questions: "Horny for status", "Sauron in our pockets", "Broken news", "SPACtacular",
"What's your enough?", "History is just one fucking thing after another".

## The dictated voice

### Dictated essays

When you speak an essay, the sentences grow. A fifth of them pass 30 words and one in sixteen passes
45. Commas nearly double (1.6 per sentence), paragraphs run to five sentences, and the clauses chain
with "and then" (15 per 10,000 words). This is the "long wave then short break" voice the earlier
profile described, and it is real, but it belongs to dictation. The oral connectors live here too
and nowhere in the written notes: "which is to say" (3.3 per 10,000), "so on and so forth" (3.7),
"quote-unquote" (3.3), "you know" (20.5), "I think" (21.7), "very" (25), "really" (22.6).

> Blood must be paid by blood, which means Orestes must die, which means someone must kill his killer,
> and someone must kill that killer, an infinite regress of vengeance.

The em dashes in the dictated transcripts (4.1 per 1,000 words) mostly come from the transcription
and cleanup step, which writes false starts and asides as dashes.

### Working prompts

To tools you talk in questions (24% of sentences) and short instructions, open with "Also" (281
sentences), and ask for opinions constantly: "Do you think" 148 times, "does it make sense to" 34,
"I need your help" 55, "I don't know if" 51. Your working vocabulary is a design brief: cleanly,
elegant, opinionated, world-class, no bloat, no gimmicks, no slop. Typed prompts carry typos ("thsi",
"waht", "cehck"); dictated ones carry garbles (listed in `dictationGarbles`).

### What AI polishing did to your essays

Comparing the six AI-assisted finished essays of 2026 with your raw dictation and your written notes
shows what the polish adds:

| | Written notes | Raw dictation | AI-polished essays | Model-written notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Em dashes per 1,000 words | 2.2 | 4.1 | 6.2 | 14.1 |
| Sentences of 6 words or fewer | 10.8% | 12.6% | 20.1% | 30.5% |
| Staccato runs per 10,000 words | 0.4 | 1.2 | 2.2 | 5.3 |
| "actually" per 10,000 words | 1.0 | 4.9 | 10.9 | 33 |
| "quiet/quietly" per 10,000 words | 0.2 | 0 | 2.2 | 1.8 |

The polish roughly triples your dash rate, doubles your short sentences, and adds the clipped
fragments you yourself named as the problem: "overly smoothed prose that removes the roughness of my
voice" and "the staccato one-line paragraph patterns".

## Your own tics to watch

These are your habits that weaken the prose. Counts are from the 134k words of written notes.

1. **Praise in place of detail.** brilliant, fascinating, interesting, amazing, wonderful, beautiful
   and delightful together appear 424 times, one every 315 words, with "profound" another 45. "A
   brilliant post" (8 times), "a brilliant article" (5), "this wonderful post". The note is stronger
   when the detail does the praising.
2. **Intensifiers.** bloody (16), utterly (10), remarkably (8), absolutely (8), truly (7), incredibly
   (5), insanely, ridiculously: 60 in all.
3. **Superlative hedges.** "one of the" 111 times, "one of the most" 25, "one of my favorite" 14,
   "one of the greatest" 11.
4. **Padding phrases.** "the fact that" 49, "for a long time" 32, "a lot of" 72, "it goes without
   saying that" 9 (followed, every time, by saying it), "just" 253 (about twice the general rate).
5. **Apologising for the post.** 6 of 62 posts open by calling themselves messy, rambling, a stream of
   consciousness or a permanent draft.
6. **The same doorway.** 28 of 62 openings are some version of "I came across" or "this popped up
   on my feed". It is honest and it is yours, and the blunt openers ("Everything is cyclical.") are
   the ones readers remember.
7. **In dictation only:** "you know", "I think", "very", "really", "and then" chains, "so on and so
   forth". Cleanup may delete the fillers; the rest is yours to cut when you edit.

## AI patterns to avoid like the plague

These are the patterns that make prose read as machine-written. Each one was checked against your
corpus, and the list keeps only what you almost never do. The rates compare your written notes with
the model-written contrast set, per 10,000 words. Across all of them, your notes trip the detector
2.3 times per 10,000 words and the model-written notes 85 times, roughly 37 times as often.

`checkStyle` (style.ts) already blocks em dashes, the short "not X but Y", filler hedges, the
banned-word list, URLs, exclamation marks and "Moreover"-style openers. `voiceCheck` (voice.ts)
adds the following.

| Pattern | Bad example (real model output where possible) | Why it reads as machine | You | Machine |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Staccato run | "Six words that contain an entire philosophy. Write hard. Write clear. Write about what hurts." | Rhythm applied from outside. You land one short sentence, then move on. | 0.4 | 5.3 |
| Mechanical anaphora | "Read it when you're stuck. Read it when you're afraid. Read it when you've forgotten why you started." | A sermon template. | 0.2 | 7.1 |
| "Not a X. A Y." | "Not a rant. A diagnosis." | Corrects a position nobody held. | 0 | 3.5 |
| "Doesn't just X, he Y" | "He doesn't just mourn the old web, he shows you exactly what we lost." | Escalates by denial. Your "isn't just X; it's also Y" adds a point instead. | 0.2 | 5.3 |
| "Why it matters" | "This is why curation matters." | Announces importance instead of showing a consequence. | 0.1 | 7.1 |
| Blurb cliché | "A love letter to the link." "A eulogy and a rallying cry, all at once." "This essay is the antidote." | Publicity copy; praise nobody can check. | 0.4 | 33.6 |
| Inflated stakes | "The most important thing anyone has ever said about the internet." "Secretly about everything." | Superlatives with no measure. | 0 | 8.8 |
| Signposted reveal | "Here's the part I can't wriggle out of." "The answer? Nobody." | A drum-roll before an ordinary point. You named the first one yourself. | 0.1 | 1.1 in polished essays |
| "And yet" opener | "The web we loved is gone. And yet, something survives." | Signals depth before offering any. | 0 | 3.5 |
| "Quiet/quietly" | "The quiet extinction of the web's most beloved species." | Faux restraint. | 0.2 | 1.8 |
| Possessive triad | "Reclaiming your focus, your time, and your right to be present." | Three beats built for cadence. | 0 | 1.8 |

### Where the house style check bans something you do

`checkStyle` applies only to machine prose, and these bans are right for machine prose. You should
still know that it flags your real habits, so a flagged word in your own draft is no verdict on you:

- **Em dashes.** You use about one every 460 words, unspaced, and have since before ChatGPT. Models
  use one every 70 words. The ban stays for machine text; your dashes are yours.
- **"Not X but Y".** checkStyle's pattern fires 48 times in your notes (3.6 per 10,000 words, against
  8.8 in the model notes). Most of yours are additive ("not only make us laugh but also think") and
  some open a post ("I am a hoarder, not of physical objects but of links."). The model version
  denies a straw position to sound profound. The regex cannot tell them apart, so it stays strict for
  machines.
- **Banned words you use:** profound (44 with "profoundly"), nuanced (12), thought-provoking (11),
  resonate (6), testament (5, mostly "Old Testament", a false positive), unleash (11).
- **Finance false positives:** "leverage" and "leveraged" (20) are finance terms in your writing
  (leveraged ETFs, margin). A machine note about F&O or margin trading will trip the ban for a
  legitimate reason.
- **"When it comes to"** appears 6 times in your notes and 12 in your dictation.

None of this changes style.ts. If you want the ban relaxed for a term (leverage in a finance
context is the likeliest), that is your call.

## How the Desk uses this

- `editorVoice` is a prompt block of about 230 words for models that draft suggestions you may adopt:
  title options, the route draft, the questions for the field note. It describes the curatorial
  register above and forbids the patterns in the table.
- `voiceCheck(text)` runs over machine prose after `checkStyle`. Errors can join the regeneration
  loop (`describeVoiceFindings` formats them); warnings can show beside the text.
- `dictionaryHints` lists 116 proper nouns and terms, most useful first, for the transcription
  prompt. `dictationGarbles` maps the recurring misses (Axara to Akshara, Quen to Qwen, Bhav copy to
  bhavcopy, household dent to household debt, Bogal to Bhogal).
- Your own words never go through any of this.

Wiring these into `packages/server` prompts is the next step and is not done in this change.

## What this corrects in the earlier profile

The `writing-voice` skill described a "flowing and maximalist" essay voice of long comma-built
waves, signposted turns ("But here's the twist") and mythic allusion. The numbers show that profile
was drawn from dictated and AI-polished 2026 essays. Your written curatorial notes are medium-length,
plain and conversational, with long sentences rare (4.9%), signposted twists almost absent (2 in
134k words) and myth scarce. The crude humour, first person, honesty and concrete pictures are
confirmed. The comma-over-dash claim is half right: commas outnumber dashes 25 to 1, and you still
use single em dashes steadily. The skill now carries an "Evidence check" section with these numbers.

## Methodology

**Sources, in order of weight.**

1. *Written curatorial notes.* The Dumpster Fire on Substack, from the 2025 export at
   `~/AA/Bhuvan-backups/dumpsterfire`. 64 published posts, 17 Jan 2022 to 11 Jan 2025; 62 after
   dropping two duplicate sends. Only editor-written paragraphs were kept: blockquotes, captions,
   embedded posts, tweets, headings, subscribe widgets and any paragraph that is wholly italic or a
   single quotation were removed. 134,303 words, 8,565 sentences, 3,396 paragraphs. Substack
   @-mentions were dropped by the export, so a few sentences read "by the amazing." with the name
   missing.
2. *Dictated essays.* Four raw or lightly cleaned transcripts from 2026: the Odyssey dictation, the AI
   and money dictation, the F&O voice notes and the raw notes for "What did you learn 2025". 24,639
   words. The F&O file was already lightly cleaned, which is where most of its dashes come from.
3. *Working prompts.* 1,418 unique prompts typed or dictated into Claude Code, 6 Sep to 16 Oct 2026
   (older sessions are no longer stored), 87,110 words. Kept: user turns of human origin in top-level
   sessions. Removed: tool results, system reminders, command wrappers, pasted content, task
   notifications, sub-agent prompts, code blocks, log-like lines, file paths, URLs, and untagged
   pastes detected by heavy trigram repetition. Duplicates from resumed sessions were removed.
4. *AI-polished essays* (comparison only). Six finished 2026 essays built from dictation with model
   help: Odyssey, AI and money, F&O, What did you learn 2025 (edited), Train Dreams, The Golden Age
   of Fraud. 27,379 words.
5. *Model-written contrast set.* The curator notes and blurbs on the 2026 static Dumpster Fire site
   (placeholder content written by a model; their "why you should care" notes were never the
   editor's) and one model-drafted video script from a folder of Claude drafts. 5,661 words.

**Excluded.** The 2025 to 2026 blog posts in `bhuvan-blog-theme` and `~/Documents/blogs` drafts whose
authorship could not be separated from model help; poems, public-domain texts and other people's
writing; anything quoted.

**Measures.** Sentences split on terminal punctuation with abbreviation guards; words are letter
runs. Rates are per 1,000 or 10,000 words of each set. "Distinctive words" are log ratios of corpus
frequency to general English frequency from the `wordfreq` package, ignoring words that are mostly
capitalised. Openings and closings are the first and last sentence of each post, sorted by simple
keyword rules and checked by hand. `checkStyle` and `voiceCheck` were run paragraph by paragraph over
every set.

**Limits.** The model-written contrast set is small (5.7k words), so its per-10k rates are noisy;
the patterns in the table were kept because they are near zero in 134k words of your notes. The
written corpus ends in January 2025; your curatorial voice in 2026 may have moved. Dictation
garbles marked "earlier" come from the previous profile and could not be re-checked because those
transcripts were not kept.

**Scripts.** The extraction and stats scripts were run from the session scratchpad
(`extract_chats.py`, `build_corpus.py`, `stats.py`, `vocab.py`, `moves.py`, `ends.py`,
`stylecheck.ts`); they read the sources and modify nothing.
