What cheap things cost

Coal, attention, memory machines, and a London summer that smelled so bad Parliament finally paid for sewers.

Words got cheap this year

I began the week with a book about coal from 1865 and ended it in a car, listening to Richard Hamming ask a room of Bellcore researchers why so few scientists do great work.

Somewhere between the two I noticed they were circling the same idea. When something becomes cheap, we use far more of it, and we slowly stop asking whether it was worth using. Jevons saw it with coal. Herbert Simon saw it with information. Hamming saw it with our own time.

This year it was words. Prose became nearly free to make, and you can feel it in every feed. So this first Haul is about plenty and its price, and about the few people who built tools and habits for paying attention anyway.

Start anywhere. The route below is how one thing led to the next.
