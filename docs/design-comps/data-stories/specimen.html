Fewer children, almost everywhere

An interesting paper by Michael Geruso and Dean Spears on falling fertility rates around the world. According to their data, 67% of the world’s population now lives in a country where fertility is below replacement.

The crossing

Two in three people now live where fertility is below replacement

Country by country, fertility has slipped under 2.1: Japan in 1957, the United States in 1972, China in 1991, India in 2020. Each circle is a country, sized by its people. 83 of them had crossed by 2023, home to 67 per cent of the world's people. Most of the rest cross in the UN's projection; 11 do not before 2100.

The floor

Of 51 countries that fell below 1.5, none has come back

51 countries of a million people or more have fallen below 1.5 children per woman. None has returned to replacement. The highest any has climbed back to is Moldova's 1.89. South Korea, at 0.72 in 2023, is the lowest of the large countries.

To put it bluntly, history offers no examples of societies recognizing very low birth rates as a social priority and then responding with effective changes that restore, and sustain, replacement-level fertility.

Michael Geruso and Dean Spears, “The Likelihood of Persistently Low Global Fertility”, Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2026Matched word for word
The spike

The world's births peaked in 2012

146 million babies were born in 2012, more than in any year before or, in the UN's projection, since. Deaths keep rising as the world ages, and in 2084 they overtake births. That is the year the world's population peaks, at 10.3 billion.
