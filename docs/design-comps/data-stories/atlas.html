Fewer children, almost everywhere

An interesting paper by Michael Geruso and Dean Spears on falling fertility rates around the world. According to their data, 67% of the world’s population now lives in a country where fertility is below replacement.

IIt happened everywhere

I
The fall

Today, most of the world outside sub-Saharan Africa has fallen below that line.

B., on the paper

The world went from 5.31 children per woman to 2.25 in sixty years

In 1963 the average woman could expect 5.31 children; by 2023, 2.25, just above the 2.1 at which each generation replaces the last. 158 of 161 countries ended lower than they began. In the UN's projection for 2100, half the countries end between 1.58 and 1.89, and the world goes below 2.1 in 2050; its 95 per cent range for the world in 2100 runs from 1.63 to 2.25.

II
The crossing

Two in three people now live where fertility is below replacement

Japan crossed 2.1 in 1957, the United States in 1972, China in 1991, India in 2020. Each circle is a country, sized by its people: 83 had crossed by 2023, home to 67 per cent of the world. The hollow ones cross in the UN's projection; 11 do not before 2100.

III
Twelve countries

Twelve countries, one scale

Each panel is one country against the world in grey, on one scale. The UN projects 7 of these 12 to drift up from where they are now and the rest to keep falling; by 2100 all 12 are between 1.30 and 1.91.

IIIt does not come back

IV
The floor

There have been spikes, the Baby Boom being the famous one, but they were blips around a long-term decline.

B., on the paper

Of 51 countries that fell below 1.5, none has come back

51 countries of a million people or more have gone below 1.5 children per woman. None has returned to replacement; the highest any has climbed back to is 1.89, in Moldova. South Korea, at 0.72 in 2023, is the lowest of them.

To put it bluntly, history offers no examples of societies recognizing very low birth rates as a social priority and then responding with effective changes that restore, and sustain, replacement-level fertility.

Michael Geruso and Dean Spears, “The Likelihood of Persistently Low Global Fertility”, Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2026Matched word for word

IIIWhat it adds up to

V
The peak

The world’s births peaked in 2012, in the UN’s central projection

146 million babies were born in 2012. Deaths rise as the world ages, and in 2084 they overtake births, the year the world’s population peaks at 10.3 billion. The range is wide: by 2100 the UN puts births between 85 and 155 million a year.

VI
Where babies are born

By 2100, almost one baby in two is born in Africa

In 1950, 12 of every 100 babies were born in Africa and 62 in Asia. In 2023, 35 and 50. In the UN's projection for 2100, 48 and 38.

VII
India

And yes, India as a whole is now below replacement, though, like any country, with vast regional variation.

B., on the paper

India went below replacement in 2020; its people keep growing until 2061

India's fertility fell from 5.73 in 1950 to 1.98 in 2023, crossing 2.1 29 years after China. The population goes on rising because so many Indians are young: in the UN's central projection it peaks in 2061 at 1.70 billion.

VIII
Why India keeps growing

Fewer babies, but more parents

In 2023 India had 115 million children under five and 128 million aged 15 to 19: the base of its pyramid has already narrowed. The bulge of young adults carries the population up for another 38 years. By 2061 the largest age group is 55 to 59.
