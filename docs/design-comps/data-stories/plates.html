The grammar: Plates
  1. Each figure is a numbered plate inside a double plate mark, as in a nineteenth-century statistical atlas: salvaged from the archive, like the magazine.
  2. Hatching means "not yet": the UN's projections are engraved in fine hatching and dashes; what has happened is solid.
  3. Ink and greys only. The Haul's ink is the story; every other country is a grey line or circle behind it.
  4. Plates run across the margin and the column; the machine's words sit in the column above, in the blue sans.
  5. Notes on the plate are short, in the machine's blue, with a hairline leader to the point they mean.

Fewer children, almost everywhere

An interesting paper by Michael Geruso and Dean Spears on falling fertility rates around the world. According to their data, 67% of the world’s population now lives in a country where fertility is below replacement.

Chosen by Bhuvanesh · Words and figures drawn by the machine from UN data, every number checked against the rows

Plate I · The fall

In sixty years the world went from 5.31 children per woman to 2.25

In 1963 the average woman in the world could expect to have 5.31 children. By 2023 it was 2.25, just above the 2.1 at which each generation replaces the one before. The fall reached almost every country at once. In the UN's central projection the world itself goes below 2.1 in 2050.

Plate II · The crossing

Two in three people now live where fertility is below replacement

Country by country, fertility has slipped under 2.1: Japan in 1957, the United States in 1972, China in 1991, India in 2020. Each circle is a country, sized by its people. 83 of them had crossed by 2023, home to 67 per cent of the world's people. Most of the rest cross in the UN's projection; 11 do not before 2100.

Plate III · The floor

Of 51 countries that fell below 1.5, none has come back

51 countries of a million people or more have fallen below 1.5 children per woman. None has returned to replacement. The highest any has climbed back to is Moldova's 1.89. South Korea, at 0.72 in 2023, is the lowest of the large countries.

To put it bluntly, history offers no examples of societies recognizing very low birth rates as a social priority and then responding with effective changes that restore, and sustain, replacement-level fertility.

Michael Geruso and Dean Spears, “The Likelihood of Persistently Low Global Fertility”, Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2026Matched word for word

Plate IV · At that rate

At 0.72, each generation is about a third the size of the last

If South Korea's rate held, 100 adults would have about 34 children and 12 grandchildren. This is arithmetic on one number, not a forecast: each generation is the last multiplied by 0.72 over 2.1.

Plate V · The spike

The world's births peaked in 2012

146 million babies were born in 2012, more than in any year before or, in the UN's projection, since. Deaths keep rising as the world ages, and in 2084 they overtake births. That is the year the world's population peaks, at 10.3 billion.

Plate VI · Where babies are born

By 2100, almost one baby in two is born in Africa

In 1950, 12 of every 100 babies were born in Africa and 62 in Asia. In 2023 it was 35 and 50. In the UN's projection for 2100, 48 and 38.

Plate VII · India

India went below replacement in 2020; its people keep growing until 2061

India's fertility fell from 5.73 in 1950 to 1.98 in 2023, crossing 2.1 in 2020, 29 years after China. Because so many Indians are young, the population goes on rising: in the UN's projection it peaks in 2061 at 1.70 billion.
