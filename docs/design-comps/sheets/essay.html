The Atlantic, July 1945

As We May Think

Vannevar Bush

Everyone cites the memex and almost nobody reads the essay. Read it now, while generated text piles up around us, and it stops being a period piece. Bush wasn't worried about storage. He was worried about finding the one thing that mattered in a pile that kept growing.

B.

The summation of human experience is being expanded at a prodigious rate, and the means we use for threading through the consequent maze to the momentarily important item is the same as was used in the days of square-rigged ships.

Vannevar Bush, As We May Think, Section 1

The sourceThe AtlanticJuly 1945 · 40 minutes

Published in July 1945, while Bush still ran the wartime Office of Scientific Research and Development. The war was ending, and he was asking what scientists should turn to next. Wikipedia: Vannevar Bush

Douglas Engelbart read it as a young Navy radar technician in the Philippines. Two decades later he gave the demonstration that introduced the mouse and hypertext. Wikipedia: Douglas Engelbart

From Wikipedia

  • Memex. The desk-sized machine Bush imagined for storing a person's books and records and linking them by trails.
  • Vannevar Bush. Engineer who organised American wartime science and argued for a postwar National Science Foundation.
  • The Mother of All Demos. Engelbart's 1968 demonstration in San Francisco, where Bush's trails became something you could click.

Cross-references

  • Answers: The Coal Question, 1865. Jevons explains why there is always more of a cheap thing. Bush proposes a machine for living with the excess.
  • Same problem, later: Herbert Simon, 1971. “A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention.” Simon names the cost Bush was designing around.
  • Same mechanism: Attention Is All You Need, 2017. Both retrieve by association instead of by index. Bush's trails join records the way attention joins words.
